# PearlQuant is a Blackwell-only blog post, not a tool

PearlQuant is real, but it is not usable by this project. Pearl Research Labs published one blog post, with no code, no paper, no listed authors and no date. The post describes an E8-lattice vector quantizer for the KV cache of GLM-5.3 running in vLLM on an 8×B200 node. Its headline claim is **3.2 bits per value, 65% less KV storage than an FP8 baseline, with benchmark parity**. I fetched the URL and confirmed the post exists, which settles the disagreement between the notes: `apple_silicon_kv_landscape.md` never opened Pearl's site (its own gaps section says so), while `pearl_method.md` did. The numbers are self-reported on one model, and the only quality baseline is FP8. No independent reproduction exists. Nothing in it targets llama.cpp, Metal or a dense 4B model. What carries over is the lesson, not the code. Rotation plus low-bit KV quantization can be near-lossless when the quantizer is good. llama.cpp already ships a rotation path (PR #21038) that this project may or may not have in its build. The useful work is therefore testing the existing q8_0/q4_0 KV types, with rotation, across loop counts. That test is cheap, about 10 to 20 hours of Mac GPU time. It also answers an open question for a looped model: whether loops amplify KV error.

## What the post says and what it leaves out

I fetched [the post](https://pearlresearch.ai/research/blog/pearlquant) directly (through a fetch summarizer, not a raw page view). It describes PearlQuant as "NestQuant inspired". Groups of eight values are rounded together to the nearest codeword on the E8 lattice, after a random rotation that makes values look Gaussian. NestQuant is [arXiv 2502.09720](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09720v1). The authors wrote a CuTe DSL attention kernel for Blackwell that reads the compressed cache directly and decodes codewords on chip ([PearlQuant post](https://pearlresearch.ai/research/blog/pearlquant)).

The reported numbers are as follows.

| Item | FP8 | PQ-3.2 | PQ-2.3 | PQ-2.1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Average bits per value | 9.1 | 3.2 | 2.3 | 2.1 |
| KL vs FP8 on PG-19 (FP8 noise floor ~0.04) | n/a | 0.057 | 0.122 | 0.207 |
| RULER | 97.2 | 97.1 | 96.5 | 94.8 |
| LongBench v2 | 69.2 | 68.2 | 69.0 | 60.4 |
| MMLU-Pro | 86.3 | 86.1 | 85.5 | 85.7 |
| LiveCodeBench | 95.7 | 94.9 | 93.6 | 95.2 |

A 100k-token GLM-5.3 session needs 5.1 GB of KV at FP8 and 1.8 GB with PearlQuant. Under a replayed Claude Code trace at 128 concurrent sessions, p90 time to first token was about 144 s for FP8, 81 s for NVFP4 and 7.3 s for PearlQuant. Peak throughput was 24% higher than FP8, and FP8 was about 8% faster at light load ([PearlQuant post](https://pearlresearch.ai/research/blog/pearlquant)). A theoretical "wasted bits" comparison on Gaussian data puts PearlQuant at 0.39 to 0.42, TurboQuant at 0.56 to 0.60, NVFP4 at 1.15 and FP8 at 2.65.

The post leaves out most of what an implementer would need: per-channel versus per-token layout, key versus value treatment, outlier handling, calibration, and any full-precision window. It also gives no single-stream decode speed and no dense-model or Apple silicon results. Its own stress configs show damage. PQ-2.1 drops LongBench v2 by 8.8 points, and a blind self-judge blamed it 69.7% of the time (95% CI 66 to 73) against 52.5% for a second FP8 run. The "Wait" token rate rises 48% to 106% at PQ-2.1 ([PearlQuant post](https://pearlresearch.ai/research/blog/pearlquant)). Only the 3.2-bit config reaches parity.

## Credibility is moderate on quality and weak on speed

The quality evidence is better than a typical vendor blog. The post reports a noise floor for FP8-versus-FP8 runs, multiple runs per LiveCodeBench problem, a stress ladder that shows where the method breaks, and a text-behavior check beyond accuracy. Those are the habits of people who understand that KL divergence and benchmark means can hide damage. The company background is genuine. Pearl Research Labs runs the Pearl proof-of-useful-work chain, has a Together AI partnership, and posts other kernel write-ups ([pearlresearch.ai](https://pearlresearch.ai); [Together AI](https://www.together.ai/blog/together-ai-partners-with-pearl-research-labs)). It is not a university group and has no peer-reviewed KV paper.

Four things limit the claims. First, everything is on GLM-5.3, a sparse-attention model with separate NoPE and RoPE components, which is a very different structure from Dory's 4 KV heads of dimension 256. Second, the headline "65% less" is measured against a 9.1-bit mixed FP8/BF16 baseline. Against our f16 cache, 3.2 bits would be 80% less (3.2/16 = 0.2, my calculation), and against q4_0 at 4.5 bits it would be about 29% less. Third, the 144 s versus 7.3 s time-to-first-token gap is a queueing effect from fitting more sessions in memory, not faster decoding. At light load FP8 won. Fourth, quality parity is checked only against FP8. KIVI, KVQuant, QuaRot and llama.cpp's own types are absent, and TurboQuant appears only as a theoretical curve because its kernels do not support GLM-5.3's attention. No one outside the lab has reproduced any of it, according to the searches in the notes. Treat it as a plausible, unverified design.

## Usability: no code, no Metal, and a cheaper route already exists

PearlQuant itself has no released code. The lab's public GitHub organization holds four repos (the `pearl` blockchain monorepo, a verification helper, a proposals repo, and a Trezor blockbook fork), and none is a KV or quantization project ([github.com/pearl-research-labs](https://github.com/pearl-research-labs)). The kernel is written for Blackwell tensor cores, the serving stack is vLLM with LMCache, and nothing is ported to llama.cpp, MLX or Metal. Reimplementing an E8 decoder in Metal from a blog summary, with undisclosed scale handling, would be a research project, not an experiment. Do not budget GPU hours for it.

The practical alternatives already exist in llama.cpp, with the status below taken from the notes. Quantized KV on Metal needs flash attention, and Metal support became real with [PR #27390](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/27390), merged Aug 20, 2026, which dequantizes K and V to an F16 scratch buffer before the flash-attention kernel. That PR measurably cut decode speed on an M4 Max (216 to 144 tok/s at depth 8192 on a 0.6B model). A follow-up, #27438, disables the dequant path for small batches. I did not open #27438 itself, and the notes found no post-fix M4 decode numbers ([PR #27390](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/27390)). [PR #21038](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/21038) adds a Hadamard rotation before KV storage, merged Apr 1, 2026. On gpt-oss-20b with AIME25, rotation lifted q4_0 KV from 2.0% to 21.7% and q8_0 from 31.7% to 37.1%. That shows un-rotated q4_0 can wreck long reasoning, and even rotated q4_0 stayed far below q8_0. Mixed K/V types such as q8_0 K with q4_0 V failed on Metal in one report ([issue #21450](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/21450)), and no one has verified them after #27390. This project's llama.cpp is an unmerged Dory branch (PR #30306), and whether it includes #27390, #27438 or #21038 is unknown. That is the first action item, not a fact. Rotation in particular is documented as unsupported for MLA models, and nobody has said whether the Dory attention path calls it.

## What KV memory actually costs on Dory

The calculation below uses project measurements for the architecture. The 4 KV heads, head dimension 256 and 8 + 20k + 8 attention instances come from the controller work, not from a source I could fetch. The llama.cpp PR text quotes 16 + 40k + 16 layers ([PR #30306](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/30306)). That is consistent with 36 attention half-blocks plus 36 MLP half-blocks at k=1, which is my reading, so I use the project's attention counts.

Calculation, f16: 2 (K and V) × 4 heads × 256 dim × 2 B = 4,096 B, or 4 KB per attention-instance per token. At k=1 that is 36 instances, about 144 KB per token. At k=4 it is 8 + 80 + 8 = 96 instances, about 384 KB per token. Using ggml block layouts of 8.5 bits for q8_0 and 4.5 bits for q4_0 (a ratio of 0.53 and 0.28, my calculation, not verified against llama.cpp source):

| KV type | k=1 per token | k=4 per token | k=4, 4 slots, 2,048 positions each |
|---|---|---|---|
| f16 | 144 KB | 384 KB | about 3.1 GB |
| q8_0 | about 77 KB | about 204 KB | about 1.7 GB |
| q4_0 | about 40 KB | about 108 KB | about 0.9 GB |

Two consequences follow. First, KV is smaller than the earlier notes assumed. The previous estimate attributed 7 to 8 GB of the measured 11 GB (project measurement, k=4, 4 slots) to KV. At 204 KB per token for q8_0, 8 GB of KV would mean about 39,000 token positions across four slots, roughly 10,000 per slot. Either the server was launched with a large per-slot context, which llama.cpp allocates up front whether or not it is used (general llama.cpp behavior, not from the notes), or much of the 11 GB is compute buffers and weights. The fix is to read the KV buffer size line in the server log. Second, if context is about 2,048 per slot, a 1024-token output adds only a few hundred MB, so **KV quantization may not be the memory bottleneck for the 1024-token rerun at all**. Sliding-window layers (3 of every 4, window 1024) could cap their share if llama.cpp uses a window-sized cache for them, which needs checking in the log. Because the user will not change `iogpu.wired_limit_mb`, the usable GPU budget is the default cap, commonly about two-thirds of 24 GB (from general knowledge; confirm with `sysctl iogpu.wired_limit_mb`). The 11 GB figure leaves room. Memory only becomes binding with many more slots or much longer context.

## Do loops amplify KV error? Untested, and the decision rule is clear

The project measured q8_0 KV costing 1.5 accuracy points on the 300-item holdout (Q5_K_M weights, greedy). That is larger than the near-zero q8_0 deltas in the notes, such as a KL of 0.0016 and 98.6% top-1 agreement on a 35B MoE on Metal ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/defilan/turboquant-on-a-macbook-pro-part-2-perplexity-kl-divergence-and-asymmetric-kv-on-m5-max-1gb1)). The statistical weight is thin: 1.5 points on 300 items is 4 to 5 items, so it could be noise, a rotation-or-build effect, or real amplification. The literature points both ways. LoopQ finds errors "propagate along the recurrent trajectory and are amplified" in looped models, for weights and activations ([LoopQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16343)). KVarN finds KV error accumulating across depth and decoding steps ([KVarN](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.03458)). FlashLoop argues inter-loop KV residuals get easier to quantize ([FlashLoop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29812)). No paper measures KV-quantization error against loop count on Dory, Ouro or Huginn. The test is a paired grid of KV type against k on the same items. If the q8_0 loss grows from k=1 to k=4, loops amplify error and the answer is to keep f16 at high k. If it is flat, the hypothesis dies and q8_0 is a free memory saving.

Separate from quantization, `last_loop` KV mode in the PR keeps only the final loop's cache for history, and the author says it "changes predictions compared with separate caches" ([PR #30306](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/30306)). On Ouro, shared caches did not reproduce as near-lossless ([CDB](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.09444v2)). Measure quantization and cache mode separately.

## Conclusion

PearlQuant changes what is worth trying, not what is available. Its evidence, even taken at face value, shows only that about 3.2 bits per value with good rotation and vector quantization can match FP8 on one sparse-attention MoE at 100k-token contexts. It says nothing about a 4B looped model with 96 cache instances per token at k=4. The nearest thing this project can run, rotated q4_0 KV in llama.cpp, has shown severe long-reasoning loss in one third-party test. So q4 should be treated as guilty until the paired k=1 versus k=4 grid clears it.

The calculation also shifts the priority. Dory's KV is small per token, 144 to 384 KB in f16, so with moderate per-slot context the 1024-token rerun may fit without any quantization. Check the real KV buffer first (experiment 1). The 512-token truncation of about a third of answers is a larger and cheaper accuracy problem than the 1.5-point KV cost. Quantized KV earns its place only if memory is shown to bind, or if the k-by-KV grid shows the loss does not grow with loops.
